---
title: Methodology Overview
description: Why Primi measures momentum rather than price, and the logic behind the excess momentum approach.
---

The Primi index for each listed company measures the *rate of change* of that company's secondary market composite midpoint relative to its own recent history — not the price level itself. You are trading momentum and trajectory, not absolute valuation.

## Why not track price directly?

The most obvious approach is to track the secondary market price level. If a company's composite midpoint is $200 today, the index is 200. If it rises to $220, the index is 220. The problem is that for large, successful private companies, the direction is not in question. Everyone knows companies like Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX are worth more today than two years ago and will likely be worth more two years from now.

A price level index for these companies is a one-sided market. Almost everyone wants to be long and almost nobody wants to be short. That is not a market.

## Why not track momentum alone?

A pure momentum index partially addresses this but still has an upward bias for companies in sustained high-growth phases. If a company has been appreciating at 80 percent per year consistently, even a momentum index reflecting that rate keeps rising. The genuinely uncertain question is not where a company is valued or how fast it is appreciating — it is whether it is appreciating *faster or slower than its own recent history*.

## Excess momentum over a personal baseline

Primi measures the excess of current 60-day momentum over each company's own trailing 12-month average rate of change. This is the signal.

- When current momentum exceeds the baseline, the index rises.
- When it falls below the baseline, the index falls.
- When current momentum exactly matches the baseline, the index stays flat — regardless of whether the absolute price is still rising.

A company can have an excellent quarter and still see its Primi index fall if the market expected an even better quarter based on recent history. This is precisely how equity markets create two-sided trading for excellent companies: the price reflects performance relative to expectations, not performance in absolute terms.

## Precedents

This approach has established precedents. The MSCI Momentum Index captures stocks outperforming their own recent history. The VIX uses raw bid-ask quotes from independent exchanges to measure how fast the market is moving rather than where it is. Primi applies the same structure to private company secondary markets: raw bid-ask quotes from independent platforms, aggregated into a continuous momentum signal measured against each company's own history.

Momentum as a tradable signal is one of the most documented return factors in finance, validated across asset classes for decades. Applied here, it solves the directional bias problem structurally.
